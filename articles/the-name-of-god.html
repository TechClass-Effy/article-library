A Conversation Between me (the Seeker) and my study assistant on the Name Above Every Name

By Gideon Ephratah and Claude Opus 4.7

A Word Before You Begin

Friend, what you are about to read did not begin as a sermon. It began as a conversation — the kind of quiet, wondering exchange that happens when a believer sits down with an open Bible and starts asking the questions his heart has been carrying for years.

I have preserved the conversation just as it unfolded — question and answer, back and forth — because I think you will find yourself somewhere in it. The questions were not academic. They were the questions of a man who loves his God and wanted to know Him better. And as often happens when a soul comes hungry to the Scriptures, the Word opened doors that neither of us expected to walk through.

Come and walk with us. Bring your Bible. Bring your breath — you will need it before we are done.

Part One — The Old Hand — Where the Letters Were Born

The Question Under the Question

The Seeker asked: Before you show me God's Name, I have to ask a question that sits underneath all the others. Letters didn't fall from the sky. Somewhere, somebody took the first picture and made it stand for a sound — and after him the whole world learned to write. Who was he? Where did he stand? And how did Israel come by her letters? Before we open the Name, I want to meet the hand that wrote it.

Answer: That is the oldest question on the page — and the answer starts not in a temple and not in a palace, but among working men.

◆

The Seeker asked: "Who invented the alphabet? And how does anybody even do such a thing?"

Answer: Think how a mother teaches her little one letters. She doesn't start with rules. She starts with pictures that talk: A is for apple. The apple gives up the first sound of its own name — and the child has learned a letter without ever knowing he learned anything hard.

That's the whole invention — and somebody thought of it once, in one place, and the world has leaned on it ever since.

(Here is history, not Scripture — marked so, as always in this book.) Long before Moshe, by the best count of the scholars, Egypt's kings sent working men south into the cliffs of Sinai to dig turquoise. The kings kept their records in picture-writing — thousands of beautiful little pictures of gods and kings, years to learn, closed to working men. The diggers weren't scribes; they were men who needed to scratch a note quick and plain. So they made themselves a schoolbook of their own: a few dozen small pictures of ordinary things — an ox, a house, a hand, water — and they made each picture say the first sound of its own name. The ox-picture no longer meant ox; it said the sound that ox starts with. A working man could learn his letters in a day.

No name in history claims the invention. But what grew out of it claims the world — nearly every alphabet people have written since came down from that one idea, and our own letter A is still the ox's head, tipped over by three thousand years of hurry.

And here is why the story matters before any other in this book: the four letters of God's Name belong to this family. When Israel first wrote His Name, she wrote it in these old picture-letters — every letter still a little drawing, every drawing still saying something.

◆

The Seeker asked: "But did Israel write? How do we know a people like that wrote at all?"

Answer: Scripture speaks first — as it always does in this book — and it speaks of a writing people in Moshe's own lifetime:

"And the Lord said to Moses, Write this for a memorial in a book, and speak in the ears of Joshua; for I will utterly blot out the memorial of Amalec from under heaven." (Exodus 17:14, LXX, Brenton)
"And Moses wrote the words of this law in a book, and gave it to the priests the sons of Levi who bear the ark of the covenant of the Lord, and to the elders of the sons of Israel." (Deuteronomy 31:9, LXX, Brenton)
"And Joshua wrote upon the stones a copy of the law, even the law of Moses, before the children of Israel." (Joshua 8:32, LXX, Brenton)

Write. Wrote. Wrote on stones. The WORD doesn't picture a people guessing at marks. It pictures Moshe writing in his own day and Yohoshua writing on stones before all Israel — a nation with a hand for it from the beginning.

And the ground agrees.

(History, marked so.) The stones of the land answer in that same old hand: a farmer's limestone tablet of the harvest months; letters cut in the rock of a king's water-tunnel — the tunnel the WORD itself remembers by name (2 Kings 20:20); and the last clay letters of the kingdom, scratched at a gate as Babylon closed in. The scholars call these old letters by a long name — paleo-Hebrew — but the plainer working word is what we'll keep: the old letters. In the old letters Israel wrote her Book, father to son, long before any exile taught her a new form.

◆

The Seeker asked: "What's the oldest piece of God's Book we still have?"

Answer: Now I'll show you the oldest piece of the Book of God that human hands have ever held — and it isn't paper.

(History, marked so: in 1979, below the southern shoulder of Jerusalem, workers clearing a burial cave found two little rolls of silver — charms, thin as ribbon, worn and stuck together by the years — laid with a man of the last days before Babylon came. The scholars put them near six hundred years before Christ. Careful hands unrolled the silver; on it, in the old letters, stood the priestly blessing — with God's Name written all through its lines.)

"The Lord bless thee and keep thee; the Lord make his face to shine upon thee, and have mercy upon thee; the Lord lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace." (Numbers 6:24-26, LXX, Brenton)
"And they shall put my name upon the children of Israel, and I the Lord will bless them." (Numbers 6:27, LXX, Brenton)

Read the two together. The blessing God appointed carries His Name on every line — and the next verse tells why: the Name is to be put on His people. The man in the grave simply obeyed: the blessing pressed into silver and worn on the body, the way Israel wore it.

And one caution rides with the silver, because this road will need it later: it was a blessing worn, not a spell used. God appointed a blessing, not a charm of power — and the Name has never carried well used like magic.

◆

The Seeker asked: "So where did the old letters go? Are they gone?"

Answer: They lived long and lived loud — and they are not gone yet.

(History, marked so.) When Babylon carried Israel away and brought her home, the letters came home in a new form — the square hand of the empire's clerks — and that new form is our next stop. But the old pictures kept working long after: whole books were still copied in them down at Qumran; and in some scrolls written otherwise in the new hand, the scribes refused to dress God's Name down — alone in the middle of the page, those four letters keep the old pictures. Kings struck coins carrying the old letters, generations later. And to this very day a small remnant people — the Samaritans — copies the five books of Moshe in the old letters, and the old hand still rests in living fingers.

The old hand is not a museum piece. It is a pen that has never yet been put down.

◆

The Seeker asked: "But WHAT did the letters look like? Show me. Show me God's Name."

Answer: Now. This is the moment this whole part has been walking toward.

Here are the four letters, in their oldest form — as working men first drew them, and as Israel wrote them across a thousand years:

𐤉𐤄𐤅𐤄

Four little pictures. Here they are, one by one:

The old letter Its picture English letter Its name
𐤉 a hand Y yod — "hand"
𐤄 a man crying Behold! H he — "behold"
𐤅 a nail W waw — "nail"
𐤄 the man again H he — "behold"

Set them side by side in English and watch what stands there:

Y H W H — YHVH.

There it is. For the first time on these pages — the Name. Say it slowly. YHVH.

BEHOLD the hand! The first letter is the yod — an arm ending its work in an open hand. In the letters to come it will shrink to the smallest mark the pen can make — and still be named hand.

BEHOLD the man with his hands stretched toward the heavens — toward God! That is the second letter, the he — a man standing, both arms thrown up to heaven, every line of him crying Behold!

BEHOLD the nail! The third letter is the waw — a nail, a tent-peg: a head, and a shaft driven down. And the old word never lost its meaning; it went on saying nail — the very word by which the Tent's curtains hung in the sanctuary, generation after generation.

And BEHOLD the man again! The fourth letter stands up the same as the second — arms toward heaven, crying Behold one more time.

Now read them the way a working man reads his own hand:

The Hand. BEHOLD! The Nail. BEHOLD!

Even His Name foretells His Plan of Redemption!

One careful word, so this stays a gift and never becomes a trick: the pictures confirm; they are not a secret code. The WORD tells us what the Name bears — the bush, the substitute on the mountain, the Lamb. The pictures lift their hands in glad witness. They do not preach their own sermon. Keep the wonder — and let the WORD keep the meaning.

Next, the four letters in the Hebrew you read today — the square form the exiles brought home.

Part Two — The Four Letters

The Seeker asked: "Show me the four letters of the Tetragrammaton. Show them to me first as they appear in Hebrew — from left to right; and then from right to left as they are pronounced."

Answer: Most English Bibles hide the personal Name of God behind the word LORD printed in small capital letters. But every time you see LORD spelled that way in your Old Testament, the original Hebrew has four specific letters — the personal Name God gave to Moses at the burning bush.

These four letters are:

יהוה

To an English eye scanning left to right, they appear in this order:

ה ו ה י — He, Vav, He, Yod

But Hebrew is always read from right to left, so the true reading order is:

י ה ו ה — Yod, He, Vav, He

Which gives us the four English letters we know: Y-H-V-H

This Name appears in the Hebrew Scriptures nearly 7,000 times. It is the Name God chose for Himself. It is the Name He said should be remembered "to generations of generations" (Exodus 3:15). And yet, most believers today have never even seen the four letters, let alone been taught what they mean.

That changes now.

Part Three — When the Letters Themselves Are the Sound

The Seeker asked: "Does the pronunciation of the individual letters influence the way the name altogether is pronounced… like in English?"

Answer: In English, the name of a letter often has almost nothing to do with the sound it makes in a word. The letter "W" is called "double-you," but it says "wuh." The letter "H" is called "aitch," but it says "huh." If you tried to pronounce YHVH by sounding out the English letter-names — "why-aitch-vee-aitch" — you would produce nonsense.

But in Hebrew, the letters sing their own sound.

Yod (י) — the smallest letter, a single stroke — carries the sound Y, as in yes.

He (ה) — carries the sound H, a soft, breathy exhale, like a sigh.

Vav (ו) — carries the sound V (or W in ancient Hebrew), or sometimes an oh / oo vowel.

He (ה) again — another soft breath.

Speak the letters slowly in order — Yod, He, Vav, He — and something remarkable happens:

Yah… weh.

That is God's Name. Yahweh.

And now notice what has just happened in your body. Two of the four letters — both He's — are literally the sound of an exhale. You cannot speak God's Name without breathing it out.

Part Four — The Breath of Christ Upon His Disciples

The Seeker said: "I intuitively knew what you have just confirmed. This could cast light on the scriptural account of Jesus breathing on His Disciples saying 'Receive ye the Holy Ghost' — as if He was speaking the Name of God over them."

Answer: Your intuition is treading on holy ground. Let us go to the account and see.

Turn to John 20. The disciples are hiding in a locked room. The Master they had followed for three years was crucified three days ago. They are terrified. And suddenly, He is standing in the middle of them.

He shows them His wounds. He speaks peace. And then:

"He breathed on them, and saith unto them, Receive ye the Holy Spirit." — John 20:22

The Greek word for "He breathed on them" is a rare word — enephysēsen. It appears in only a handful of places in the whole Bible. And the places where it appears will make your heart stand up.

Genesis 2:7 — "And God formed the man of dust from the earth and breathed into his face a breath of life."

Same word. What God did to Adam is what Jesus did to His disciples.

Ezekiel 37:9 — In the valley of dry bones: "Come from the four winds and breathe upon these dead, and let them live."

Same word. The breath that raises skeletons is the breath Jesus gave in the upper room.

When Jesus breathed on His disciples, He was doing four things at once:

  • Repeating what God did in Eden — imparting life
  • Fulfilling what He promised through Ezekiel — raising the dead
  • Giving the Holy Spirit
  • And — by the very physics of exhaling — sounding out the He, He of His Father's Name

The Hebrew word for Spirit is ruach, which also means breath and wind. The Greek word is pneuma, which also means breath and wind. When Jesus breathed and said "Receive Holy Spirit," the act was the sermon. The exhale was the Name.

Every breath you take, brother and sister, participates in the shape of what God is. You have been speaking His Name, silently, since the day you drew your first breath. You will speak it, silently, when you draw your last.

Part Five — The Puzzle of "Jehovah"

The Seeker said: "Men I consider greater than I — for their God-given insight — such as E.W. Bullinger and Matthew Henry, for example, have pronounced the Name as Jehovah. It puzzles me."

Answer: Your puzzlement is honest and honorable. You honor these men — as you should, for they labored faithfully in the Word and left a legacy that has fed generations. And yet you sense a discrepancy between what the Hebrew letters plainly voice (Yah-weh) and what these giants wrote (Jehovah).

Let us reason together through Scripture and history, and see whether the puzzle resolves.

There is no "J" sound in biblical Hebrew. The letter "J" did not even exist in English until about the year 1600. It started life as a decorative tail on the letter "I." Every "J" in your English Bible — Jesus, Jacob, Jerusalem, Judah, Jehovah — was originally a Y sound.

So where did "Jehovah" come from?

After the Jews returned from exile in Babylon, a tradition arose among the scribes: God's Name was too holy to say out loud. Whenever they read Scripture in public and came to YHVH, they would substitute the word Adonai (meaning "my Lord") instead.

Centuries later, when Jewish scribes called the Masoretes added vowel markings to the Hebrew text (between about 600 and 1000 AD), they did something clever. They wrote the consonants of YHVH with the vowels of Adonai — as a silent reminder to the reader: "Don't say what's written; say Adonai instead."

The vowels of Adonai are roughly ə-o-a.

When those vowels are pressed into YHVH, you get: Y-ə-H-o-V-a-H → Ye-Ho-Vah.

This was never meant to be pronounced. It was a scribal warning label.

But when Christian scholars in the Middle Ages started learning Hebrew from old manuscripts, some of them didn't know about the convention. They read the letters as one word — Yehovah — which passed into Latin as Iehovah, then into English as Jehovah.

By the time Matthew Henry (born 1662) and E.W. Bullinger (born 1837) were writing, "Jehovah" had been the standard English form for over a century. These men did not invent it. They inherited it.

And here is what we must say about them: they used the form they had, and they used it to honor God. Bullinger especially fought hard to restore God's Name to English readers, refusing to let it stay hidden behind the word "LORD." Even Bullinger himself admitted, in his Companion Bible, that "Jehovah" is a hybrid and the true form is closer to Yahweh. He kept using "Jehovah" only because it was the form his readers recognized.

They were not lying. They were being pastoral.

But when we look at what Scripture itself preserves, the true sound of the Name shines through everywhere:

  • Hallelu-Yah — "Praise Yah" — sung by the Church for two thousand years
  • Yesha-Yahu (Isaiah) — "Yah saves"
  • Yirme-Yahu (Jeremiah) — "Yah lifts up"
  • Eli-Yahu (Elijah) — "My God is Yah"
  • Yeho-shua / Yeshua (Joshua / Jesus) — "Yah is salvation"

The Name never appears as "Jeh-" in Hebrew Scripture. It always appears as "Yah-" or "Yeho-." And the early Church Fathers who transliterated it into Greek wrote it as Iaoue (Yah-oo-eh) or Iabe (Yah-veh).

So when you sing "Hallelujah," you are singing the first syllable of God's Name. Every time. Whether you knew it or not.

Honor the giants who came before you. Read Matthew Henry. Read Bullinger. Feed on their insight. But do not be afraid to say the Name the way the letters themselves say it: Yahweh. You are not contradicting them. You are standing on their shoulders — which is exactly where they hoped you would stand.

The puzzle resolves. The Name shines through.

Part Six — Why the Two Breaths Sound Different

The Seeker asked: "In the breathy parts of the Name, why is there first a soft vowel sound in the first part followed by a long vowel sound at the second part?"

Answer: Your ear has caught something real.

The first He is inside the word. It closes the syllable Yah, but it has to hand off quickly because another syllable is coming right behind it. The mouth moves on.

The second He is at the very end of the word. Nothing comes after it. It simply releases — the mouth stays open, and the breath fades into silence.

Now consider what this means physically. Speak the Name slowly:

Yah — a quick, contained breath. Like the sharp first inhale of a newborn.

weh — a slow, released breath. Like the final exhale of a saint slipping into glory.

God's Name, in the very mouth that speaks it, is shaped like the arc of a human life. Grabbed quickly at birth. Released slowly at death. Both breaths belonging to Him.

"The spirit of God is what made me, and the breath of the Almighty is what teaches me." — Job 33:4
"When you take away their spirit, they will die and return to their dust. You will send forth your spirit, and they will be created." — Psalm 104:29–30

Every breath you have ever taken has traced the shape of His Name. You cannot escape it. It is written in your lungs.

Part Seven — Not Taking His Name in Vain

The Seeker asked: "Should we not include a study concerning not taking YHVH's Name in vain?"

Answer: You are right to ask this. We cannot walk through the majesty of the Name without asking: how then are we to carry this Name?

For the Name is not a trinket. It is not a slogan. It is not a punctuation mark for our sentences. It is the self-revelation of the God who IS — the God who breathes in every lung, who was fire in the bush and wind in the upper room. To handle such a Name lightly is to handle glory carelessly. And glory carelessly handled always leaves scars.

Turn to Exodus 20:7:

"Thou shalt not take the name of the LORD thy God in vain, for the LORD will not hold him guiltless that taketh His name in vain."

Most of us were taught this meant only: "Don't use God's name as a curse word." And that is true — but it is only the shallowest end of the water. The commandment runs far deeper.

The Hebrew word for "take" is nasa — meaning to lift up, to carry, to bear. It is the same word used for carrying a burden, raising a banner, taking up an oath. It is not the verb for merely speaking. It is the verb for taking something upon yourself.

The Hebrew word for "vain" is shav — meaning emptiness, falsehood, worthlessness, nothingness.

A better rendering of the commandment reads:

"You shall not lift up, carry, or bear the Name of YHVH your God to emptiness, to falsehood, to nothingness."

Here are seven ways Scripture shows us we can violate this — and then the positive command hidden inside it.

1. By Swearing Falsely in His Name.

"You shall not swear falsely by my name, and you shall not profane the name of your God." — Leviticus 19:12

To swear "by God" and then lie is to make Him the guarantor of a falsehood. This is why Jesus taught: "Let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay" (Matthew 5:37). Let your word be so trustworthy that you never need to prop it up with God's Name.

2. By Empty Worship.

"This people draws near me with their lips… but their heart is far from me, and in vain they worship me." — Isaiah 29:13

Singing His praises while your heart wanders to grocery lists and grievances. Reciting His Name in liturgy while feeling nothing. The remedy is not to sing less. The remedy is to bring your heart to where your mouth already is.

3. By Prophesying What He Did Not Say.

"The prophets prophesy false things in my name; I did not send them, and I did not command them, and I did not speak to them." — Jeremiah 14:14

To say "thus saith the Lord" when He has not spoken is a grave offense. Better to say "I do not know" than to fabricate His Name onto the front of your opinion.

4. By Living Like the World While Wearing His Name.

This is the deepest cut — and most of us have never been taught to see it.

"They profaned my holy name in that it was said of them: 'These are the people of the Lord…'" — Ezekiel 36:20

Israel profaned God's Name not by speaking it wrongly, but by living wrongly while bearing it. Paul brings the arrow to its target:

"The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles through you." — Romans 2:24

When a believer cheats in business, when a Christian husband is harsh with his wife, when someone who claims Christ carries hatred in his heart — the Name is dragged through the mud. This is taking the Name in vain. Not the four-letter words that fly out of an angry mouth. The whole life that says "I belong to Him" while behaving as though He were not looking.

5. By Empty Familiarity.

"A son honors a father, and a slave his lord. So if I am a father, where is my glory? And if I am the Lord, where is the fear of me?" — Malachi 1:6

The priests were treating God's Name as ordinary. Yawning through the sacrifices. Familiarity, when it decays into contempt, is a slow form of taking the Name in vain.

6. By Making Merchandise of the Name.

"Thy money perish with thee, because thou hast thought that the gift of God may be purchased with money." — Acts 8:20

When His Name becomes a product — stamped on merchandise for profit, used to sell books, invoked in fundraising letters — we are near this danger. Jesus drove the moneychangers from His Father's house. If He walked into some corners of modern religion, He would still be swinging the cord.

7. By Using the Name as a Magic Formula.

"Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are ye?" — Acts 19:15

The sons of Sceva tried to use Jesus' Name as a spell. The demons were not impressed. The Name is not a magic word. To pray "in Jesus' name" is not to invoke a formula that compels God — it is to pray as one who belongs to Him, in His character, in His will, in His authority.

The Positive Command Hidden Inside

Every negative commandment contains a positive one. "Thou shalt not take His Name in vain" implies "thou shalt carry His Name in fullness — with weight, with truth, with reverence, with life."

  • Speak His Name with weight — as one who knows what breath is being spent
  • Live so your neighbors honor Him because of how you conduct yourself
  • Worship with your heart where your lips already are
  • Refuse to make merchandise of holy things
  • Approach the Name with reverence, not familiarity decayed into contempt
  • Pray in His Name as one who belongs to Him, not as one invoking a spell

And If You Have Fallen Short

For every reader who now looks back and grieves at how carelessly you have handled His Name — hear this good news:

"If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness." — 1 John 1:9

The commandment says He will not cleanse. But the Gospel says: through the blood of His Son, He does cleanse — because His Son bore in His body the guilt of every empty word we ever spoke over His Name.

The same Jesus whose Name we have profaned is the Jesus who died to purchase our forgiveness for profaning it. Take the cleansing. Then rise, and carry the Name differently from this day forward.

Part Eight — The Great "I AM"

The Seeker asked: "Now let's examine what YHVH actually means — the 'I AM' of Exodus 3. I love the 'I AMs'. I believe YHVH revealed to me that He will be whatever His people NEED Him to be: the burning bush, the rock that gave water, the columns of cloud and fire, the parting of waters, the rustling of Myrtle leaves, the Mighty rushing Wind with tongues of fire. I could go on."

Answer: We arrive now at the fountainhead. Everything the Name will ever mean flows from a single conversation between a barefoot shepherd and a Voice in a flame that would not consume.

Turn to Exodus 3. Moses stands barefoot before a bush that burns without being consumed. God has called him to lead Israel out of Egypt. Moses asks: "When I go to them, and they ask me the name of the God who sent me, what shall I say?"

And God answers:

אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה — Ehyeh Asher Ehyeh

"I AM WHO I AM." Or: "I WILL BE WHO I WILL BE." Or: "I WILL BE WHATEVER I WILL BE."

All three translations are equally faithful to the Hebrew. God deliberately spoke a phrase that opens rather than closes His self-definition.

The Name YHVH and the phrase Ehyeh come from the same Hebrew root — hayah, to be. When God speaks of Himself, He says "I AM." When we speak of Him, we say "HE IS."

God's Name is a verb.

It is not a label. It is an ongoing act. Every time it is spoken, it declares that the God who was, is, and will be is right now, in this moment, being — for His people, for His creation, for you.

Your revelation is not sentiment. This is precisely what the Hebrew grammar of Exodus 3:14 permits and invites. He does not tell Moses what He will be — He tells Moses that He will be. He reserves the specific manifestations for the specific moments of His people's need. And then, throughout Scripture, He demonstrates it — appearing precisely as needed, precisely when needed.

Let us walk through your list, and let Scripture speak.

1. The Burning Bush (Exodus 3) — Moses needed to know that a God existed who could burn without consuming, who could touch mortal things without destroying them. So YHVH became the fire that called his name.

2. The Rock That Gave Water (Exodus 17) — Israel needed water. There was none. So YHVH became a stone that poured rivers into the desert. And Paul later tells us: "that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4).

3. The Pillar of Cloud and Fire (Exodus 13) — Israel needed shade by day and light by night, and above all they needed to know which way to go. So YHVH became a moving column of cloud and a tower of flame.

4. The Parting of the Sea (Exodus 14) — Israel needed to cross where no crossing existed. Pharaoh at their back, sea before them. So YHVH became a highway of dry ground where a moment before there had been fathoms of drowning.

5. The Rustling of Leaves (1 Chronicles 14; Zechariah 1) — David needed a signal that YHVH had gone ahead into battle. So YHVH became a stirring in the treetops. When Israel needed comfort after exile, He stood among the myrtles — the fragrant tree of restoration.

6. The Mighty Rushing Wind with Tongues of Fire (Acts 2) — The infant Church needed power. So YHVH came as hurricane in an upper room and fire dancing on human heads.

Every one of these is "I will be whatever My people need." Every one of these is the "I AM" in action.

And then — most wondrous of all — the "I AM" took on flesh.

In the Gospel of John, Jesus takes the very Greek phrase used in the Greek Old Testament for God's self-naming — Ego eimi ("I AM") — and applies it to Himself:

  • "I AM the bread of life" (John 6:35) — for the hungry
  • "I AM the light of the world" (John 8:12) — for those in darkness
  • "I AM the door" (John 10:9) — for those shut out
  • "I AM the good shepherd" (John 10:11) — for the lost
  • "I AM the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25) — for the dying
  • "I AM the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6) — for the wandering
  • "I AM the true vine" (John 15:1) — for the withered

And most staggering of all:

"Verily, verily, I say unto you, before Abraham was, I AM." — John 8:58

He did not say "I was." He said "I AM" — taking the burning-bush Name onto His own lips. The Jews understood exactly what He was claiming; they picked up stones to kill Him (John 8:59).

Jesus is the "I AM" in the flesh. He is "I will be whatever My people need" — made bread for the hungry, light for the blind, door for the shut-out, shepherd for the lost, resurrection for the dying, way for the wandering, vine for the withered.

Every "I AM" of the Gospels is a fresh blossom on the tree that first sprouted at the burning bush.

Part Nine — The Great Commandment as a Great Promise

The Seeker said: "How can we not LOVE such a God?? It makes me see the command — 'Thou shalt love the Lord your God with all of your might, mind and strength' — more as a PROPHECY or a PROMISE than a commandment."

Answer: You have just spoken something the Church has too rarely dared to say aloud. Let us test it by the Word — for I believe you have touched the very heartbeat of the New Covenant.

For centuries, the command in Deuteronomy 6:5 — "Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might" — has been read as a demand. And under the Old Covenant, that reading was not wrong. The Law commanded. The people failed. The prophets wept.

And here is the tragedy of religion without regeneration: it leaves the believer trying to squeeze love for God out of a cold, stony heart of flesh. It hands you a mountain and says "climb" — but does not give you the legs. It shows you an ocean and says "cross" — but hands you no boat. Countless sincere souls have wept themselves to sleep because they could not conjure up, out of their own broken interior, the love that Sinai commanded.

That is not the whole story. That was never meant to be the whole story.

Even Moses himself, only chapters after giving the command, foretold the day when God would supply the very love He required:

"And the Lord thy God will circumcise thine heart, and the heart of thy seed, to love the Lord thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, that thou mayest live." — Deuteronomy 30:6

Jeremiah added his witness:

"I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people." — Jeremiah 31:33

And Ezekiel added his — the very verses that break the tyranny of the stony heart:

"A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh. And I will put my Spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes." — Ezekiel 36:26–27

Do you see it? The command of Deuteronomy 6 is fulfilled by the promise of Deuteronomy 30, Jeremiah 31, and Ezekiel 36. What Sinai demanded, Calvary supplied.

The New Testament makes it plain:

"The love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Spirit which is given unto us." — Romans 5:5
"We love Him, because He first loved us." — 1 John 4:19
"It is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of His good pleasure." — Philippians 2:13

The love you have for God is not something you manufactured. It is His love, poured into you by the Spirit, flowing back to Him as its natural response. You did not climb the mountain. He picked you up and carried you.

The Great Commandment, seen through the eyes of the New Covenant, is God's promise about what He will accomplish in you. He was not standing over you at Sinai with a whip. He was standing before you with a vow:

"One day, when I have finished forming you, when My Son has bled for you, when My Spirit has filled you — you will love Me with all your heart, all your soul, all your mind, all your strength. Not because you were forced. Because you were made new. Because I first loved you. Because you finally saw who I AM."

The Shema is the Father's whispered vow over every child He is bringing home.

A Closing Word to the Body of Christ

Dear brother, dear sister — if you have read this far, take a slow breath.

Feel your lungs fill. Feel the exhale leave. Notice: the very breath in your body traces the shape of God's Name.

He is Yahweh — the God who IS, and who WILL BE whatever His people need.

He was fire in a bush and water from a stone.
He was a cloud by day and a flame by night.
He was a highway through the sea and a whisper in the trees.
He was a hurricane in an upper room.
He is bread and light and door and shepherd and vine and way and resurrection.

And He is, right now, in this very moment, being whatever you need Him to be.

If you are hungry, He is bread.
If you are lost, He is shepherd.
If you are dying, He is resurrection.
If you are wandering, He is the way.
If you are hopeless, He is standing among the myrtles.
If you are dry, He is the rock that weeps rivers.
If you are trapped, He is dry ground where the sea used to be.

You cannot help but love Him. Because He has already loved you first, and His love has been poured into your heart by the Holy Spirit. The love you feel welling up inside you as you read these words is not something you produced. It is Him, in you, returning to Himself through you.

Every breath you take says His Name.

Every heartbeat is a "Yah" and a "weh" — grasped and released, grasped and released — until the day the grasp lets go for the last time and the exhale carries you home.

Praise Yah. Halleluyah.

For He is who He is.
And He will be who He will be.
And He has already been all we could ever need.
