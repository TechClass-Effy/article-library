NOTE FROM THE AUTHOR

Before We Begin — How We're Going to Study

This study is for anybody who wants to know what the Bible actually says about the Sabbath. Not what your church says. Not what your grandmother said. Not what the preacher on the radio said. What the Book of Books says.

We're going to walk straight through the Bible from Genesis forward. We're going to let God speak for Himself, and we're going to hold every man's opinion — including our own — up to that Word. If God says one thing and a man says another, God wins. Every time.

Here's how we're going to do it:

We take God at His Word. If the Bible says it plainly, that settles it. We don't need somebody with three degrees to tell us what "rest on the seventh day" means. God can talk. We can listen.

We don't build doctrine on one verse. Anytime we want to say "the Bible teaches such-and-such," we need at least two different passages saying it. That's God's own rule — "in the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word be established" (Deuteronomy 19:15; 2 Corinthians 13:1). We follow that rule.

We read the whole Bible on the subject. Not just the verses that agree with us. Not just the verses our tradition likes. All of it. If Genesis says one thing and someone claims Romans undoes it, we go read Romans in context and see if that's really what Paul said.

We look for the OLD path. Jeremiah 6:16 says, "Stand ye in the ways, and see, and ask for the old paths, where is the good way, and walk therein, and ye shall find rest for your souls." We want the road Jesus and the Apostles walked. Not the newer road built by church councils centuries later.

We set aside the traditions of men. The Lord Jesus rebuked the Pharisees hard for this — "making the word of God of no effect through your tradition" (Mark 7:13). Doesn't matter if the tradition came from a rabbi or a bishop or a pope or your favorite preacher. If it contradicts, adds to, or takes away from the Word, it goes.

We add nothing. We take away nothing. Revelation 22:18–19 is dead serious about this. What God said is what God said. We don't help Him out with our own additions.

Now — a word on which Bibles we're using, and why. This part matters, so stick with me.

The Old Testament — Why We're Using the Septuagint (LXX)

Most working Christians open their Bibles and read the King James, or the NIV, or whatever they grew up with. Those translations use the Hebrew Old Testament that was standardized by Jewish rabbis around 500–1000 AD — hundreds of years after Jesus and the Apostles. That version is called the Masoretic Text.

Here's what a lot of folks never learned: the Bible Jesus and the Apostles actually quoted from was NOT that Masoretic Hebrew. It was a Greek translation called the Septuagint.

The Septuagint (we call it the "LXX" — that's the Roman numeral 70, because tradition says 70 Jewish scholars translated it) was made from the Hebrew Scriptures around 250 BC — about 250 years before Jesus was born. It was the Bible the whole Greek-speaking Jewish world used. It was the Bible in the synagogues Paul preached in. It was the Bible the New Testament writers had open on the desk when they wrote.

Want proof? Open your New Testament and read any quotation of the Old Testament. In roughly 3 out of 4 cases, the New Testament writer is quoting the Septuagint — sometimes word for word — not the later Masoretic Hebrew. When Paul or Matthew or Peter quotes Isaiah or Psalms, they're quoting the LXX.

So when we sit down to study what God said in the Old Testament, we want to read the same Old Testament Jesus read. That's the LXX.

We'll be using two English translations of the LXX:
• NETS — the New English Translation of the Septuagint. Scholarly, careful, published by Oxford University Press. This is our main translation — we'll quote it in full for every Old Testament passage.
• LES — the Lexham English Septuagint. Also careful, from Lexham Press, sometimes reads a little smoother.

Our practice will be this: we quote the NETS in full for every passage. If the LES translates it noticeably differently — different enough that it matters — we'll bring in the LES to show you the second angle. If the two say basically the same thing, we don't waste your time reading it twice.

The New Testament — Why We're Using the Ancient Codexes

Same story, different Testament. Most modern New Testaments are translated from a mix of ancient Greek manuscripts, and mostly the translators do a good job. But there's a difference between reading a committee's decision about what the Greek says and reading the actual oldest Greek manuscripts we have.

The two oldest, most complete Greek New Testaments in existence are:
• Codex Sinaiticus — hand-copied around 330–360 AD. Found in a monastery on Mount Sinai (that's how it got the name). It's now mostly at the British Library. When you look at Sinaiticus, you're looking at Greek writing that's about 1,650 years old, copied by scribes who were only about 250 years removed from the Apostles themselves.
• Codex Vaticanus — hand-copied around 300–325 AD. Even a little older than Sinaiticus. It's kept in the Vatican Library (that's how it got its name — it's got nothing to do with Roman Catholic doctrine; it's just where it lives now).

These two codexes ("codex" just means "book," as opposed to a scroll) are the closest thing we have to the Apostles' own writings. When we quote them, we'll tell you which codex we're pulling from. You don't need to know Greek to follow along — we'll give it to you in plain English.

One note: Codex Vaticanus is missing some pages at the end. It doesn't contain the last part of Hebrews, or the Pastoral Epistles, or Revelation. So for those books, we'll rely on Sinaiticus alone.

Why This Matters for you and me.

You might be thinking, "Look, I just want to know what God said. Why all this codex business?"

Fair question. Here's why.

If a used-car salesman tells you a truck has 50,000 miles on it, you check the odometer yourself. You don't just take his word. Same with the Bible. There are folks who will tell you the Sabbath is done away, and they'll quote you a translation of a translation of a manuscript that's been through committee decisions. We're going to skip the middlemen where we can, and read the oldest, best copies we've got.

You wouldn't trust your salvation to a used-car salesman. Don't trust it to a translation committee either. Go to the source.

The Question We're Trying to Answer

Here's what we want to know when we're done:

What does a believer today have to do to "remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy"?

Because there's no Temple standing in Jerusalem. There's no Levite priesthood offering sacrifices. Those parts of the Old Testament law required a Temple and priests to work at all. Nobody today can offer a burnt offering, because there's no altar to offer it on.

So what's left? What did God command that still applies right now, in your house, in your work, in your family, this week?

That's what we're going to figure out. Straight from the Book. Two witnesses or more for anything we say is doctrine. And we'll walk it through from Genesis forward.

Ready? Let's open the Book of Books.
